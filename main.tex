\section{Introduction}

Diabetes is one of the most common chronic diseases in the world, affecting an estimated 589~million adults aged 20--79 years in 2024 and is projected to reach 853~million by 2050~\citep{IDF2025Atlas,Genitsaridi2026Lancet}. Beyond well-recognized microvascular complications such as retinopathy and neuropathy, diabetes also confers a substantially increased risk of fragility (low-trauma) fracture. Patients with diabetes have a 2- to 5-fold higher risk of hip fracture than those without diabetes~\citep{Schwartz2001JCEM,Vestergaard2005Diabetologia,Giangregorio2012JBMR,Sellmeyer2016Diabetes,Janghorbani2006DiabetesCare}, and after long-term follow-up, approximately half of all patients with diabetes will sustain a fracture~\citep{Melton2008JBMR}. The excess fracture risk is multifactorial, reflecting a combination of higher fall rates, reduced bone turnover, and impaired bone material properties~\citep{Pijpers2012AgeAgeing,Hygum2017EJE,Furst2016JCEM}.

Despite this elevated risk, it remains difficult to identify which patients with diabetes will fracture and many are never diagnosed with fracture risk. The Fracture Risk Assessment Tool (FRAX) is the most widely used prediction tool and combines demographic, anthropometric, and clinical risk factors to estimate the 10-year probability of fracture~\citep{Kanis2010FRAX}. However, FRAX requires input that is typically obtained through a direct patient interview, which demands clinician time, and in practice it is most often calculated only after bone mineral density testing. Moreover, FRAX has only moderate discriminatory ability for hip fracture (AUROC $\approx 0.78$) and has been repeatedly shown to underestimate the risk of hip fracture in patients with diabetes~\citep{Giangregorio2012JBMR,Beaudoin2019OsteoInt,Jain2025JBMR}.

Diabetes is a heterogeneous disease whose fracture risk factors may differ from those of the general population, suggesting that disease-specific prediction tools are warranted. Advances in predictive modeling could enable targeted bone-health screening for patients with diabetes without requiring additional clinician time or bone mineral density testing. However, to date, only a small number of studies have examined machine-learning approaches to fracture prediction in this population~\citep{Li2023JOSR}. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to develop and validate a machine-learning model for the prediction of hip fractures in patients with diabetes using a large, heterogeneous and generalizable data source. Specifically, we trained models on the Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims Datasets and externally validated them with analysis of data accumulated by the National Institutes of Health \textit{All of Us} Research Program.

\section{Methods}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Design and Overview}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the IBM MarketScan Commercial Claims Database to develop and internally validate three machine-learning models for the prediction of hip fracture in patients with diabetes. The prediction task was framed as a four-class classification, in which patients were stratified by the time elapsed between the first recorded diagnosis of diabetes and the first recorded hip fracture (or assigned to a reference class if no diabetes-attributable fracture was observed within ten years).

The three compared models were: (1) BEHRT-LoRA, a BERT adapted transformer encoder for electronic health records~\citep{Devlin2019BERT,Li2020BEHRT}, pre-trained in the entire MarketScan corpus and fine-tuned for classification using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\citep{Hu2022LoRA}; (2) XGBoost, a gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble~\citep{Chen2016XGBoost}; and (3) a feedforward neural network (\texttt{SparseClassifier}). All three models were then applied, without retraining, to an independent external validation cohort drawn from the \textit{All of Us} Research Program~\citep{AllofUs2019NEJM}, allowing us to assess generalization to a more demographically diverse population.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data Sources}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{IBM MarketScan Commercial Claims Database}

The MarketScan Commercial Claims Database contains de-identified administrative claims from employer-sponsored health plans across the United States. The version used in this study covers patients enrolled between January 2003 and January 2024. Data elements included hospital and outpatient diagnosis codes (ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM), continuous enrollment records, and patient demographic information.

\subsubsection{All of Us Research Program}

The \textit{All of Us} Research Program is a prospective longitudinal cohort funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) that has enrolled U.S.\ residents aged 18 years or older from more than 340 recruitment sites throughout the country since May 2018~\citep{AllofUs2019NEJM}. The program explicitly targets historically underrepresented groups in biomedical research, including racial and ethnic minorities and individuals with limited access to health care~\citep{Ramirez2022AllofUs}.

Participant data are organized into a Curated Data Repository (CDR) with three access tiers: Public, Registered, and Controlled. We used the Controlled Tier CDR~v8 (dataset version \texttt{C2024Q3R8}), accessed via the All of Us Researcher Workbench. This release includes data from participants enrolled through October~1, 2023.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Cohort and Inclusion Criteria}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The MarketScan study cohort was constructed by linking three administrative tables and retaining only patients who met all eligibility criteria simultaneously:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Enrollment.} At least one full year of continuous insurance coverage (138{,}879{,}229 eligible enrollment records).
\item \textbf{Demographics.} Age $\geq 20$ years at the start of the first enrollment interval (179{,}415{,}164 eligible demographic records).
\item \textbf{Diagnosis.} At least one claim was assigned to a diabetes-specific PhecodeX code~\citep{Shuey2023PhecodeX}: \texttt{EM\_202} (type~2 diabetes mellitus), \texttt{EM\_202.1}, \texttt{EM\_202.2}, or \texttt{BI\_181} (type~1 diabetes mellitus) (18{,} 110 {,}999 eligible patients).
\end{itemize}

The inner join of the three tables yielded a final study cohort of $N = 12{,}174{,}785$ patients.

\paragraph{Outcome definition and risk-group assignment.}
For each patient, the date of the diabetes index $t_{\mathrm{DM}}$ was defined as the date of the first recorded diabetes diagnosis. The hip fracture was identified using the PhecodeX codes \texttt{MS\_745.11} (fracture of the femur) and \texttt{MS\_745.9} (pathological fracture); the date of the outcome index $t_{\mathrm{HF}}$ was the date of the first such code. Let $\Delta t = t_{\mathrm{HF}} - t_{\mathrm{DM}}$ (in days) be recorded when a fracture was recorded after the date of the index diabetes, and let $\Delta t = \infty$ be otherwise. The patients were assigned to one of the four mutually exclusive risk groups:
\begin{equation}
y =
\begin{cases}
1, & 1 \leq \Delta t \leq 365 \quad (<\!1\text{ year}), \\[2pt]
2, & 366 \leq \Delta t \leq 1{,}825 \quad (1\text{--}5\text{ years}), \\[2pt]
3, & 1{,}826 \leq \Delta t \leq 3{,}650 \quad (5\text{--}10\text{ years}), \\[2pt]
0, & \text{otherwise (control).}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:risk-groups}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the control class aggregates patients without recorded fractures, fractures occurring on or before $t_{\mathrm{DM}}$, and fractures occurring more than ten years after $t_{\mathrm{DM}}$. We collapsed these into a single reference group because hip fracture is a rare outcome and a gap that exceeds ten years makes a causal relationship with diabetes biologically implausible. The cohort counts for each group are given in Table~\ref{tab:groups}.

\subsection{All of Us Validation Cohort}

The same diabetes diagnosis and minimum-age criteria were applied to the All of Us CDR dataset~v8 (\texttt{C2024Q3R8}), which produced a validation cohort of 98{,}399 participants with a documented diabetes diagnosis and at least one electronic health record (EHR) condition record. Continuous enrollment was not required, because All of Us is a prospective research cohort rather than an insurance claims dataset. All cohort construction was performed in BigQuery within the All of Us Researcher Workbench.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Feature Representation}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA: EHR Token Sequences}

\paragraph{Code mapping.}
The raw diagnosis codes ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM were mapped to PhecodeX phenotypes~\citep{Shuey2023PhecodeX}, which group semantically related ICD codes into a single clinical concept. This mapping reduces vocabulary size and improves portability between ICD versions and between data sources.

\paragraph{Sequence construction.}
For each patient, all diagnosis events were sorted chronologically and restricted to those that occurred strictly before $t_{\mathrm{HF}}$. The two phenotypes of hip-fracture (MS \texttt{\_745.11} and \texttt{MS\_745.9}) were excluded from the input sequence to prevent leakage of the outcome in both control and case patients. Events occurring on the same calendar day were grouped into a single visit; a separator token (\texttt{SEP}) was inserted between consecutive visits and appended at the end of each record. The age of each event was calculated as the difference between the calendar year of the event and the patient's birth year. Sex was treated as a static patient-level attribute and assigned uniformly across all positions in the sequence.

\paragraph{Token vocabularies and embeddings.}
Each input element in a patient's history, a diagnosis, an age, or a sex label, is referred to as a \textit{token}. Unlike a text corpus, an EHR sequence contains several heterogeneous token types, each drawn from its own vocabulary (Table~\ref{tab:vocabularies}).

A transformer cannot operate on tokens directly; each token must be expressed as a fixed-length real-valued vector, called an \textit{embedding}. For every entry in each vocabulary, the model maintains one such vector. These vectors are not specified in advance; they are initialized randomly and updated during training so that tokens with similar clinical or contextual meaning come to occupy nearby regions of the embedding space.

Two embeddings encode structural rather than clinical information. The \textit{segment embedding} indicates which visit a diagnosis belongs to: it alternates between two values across visit boundaries, so that all diagnoses within the same visit share the same segment value. The \textit{position embedding} indicates the order of visits: it increments once per visit rather than once per diagnosis, so that the model attends to the sequence of visits rather than the sequence of individual codes.

At each position $i$ in the sequence, the five embedding vectors are summed to form a single combined input vector:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_i \;=\; \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{phen}}(p_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{age}}(a_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{sex}}(s_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{seg}}(g_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{pos}}(v_i),
\label{eq:embedding-sum}
\end{equation}
where $p_i, a_i, s_i, g_i, v_i$ denote the phenotype, age, sex, segment, and position tokens at position $i$, and each $\mathbf{E}_{(\cdot)}$ is a learnable lookup table that maps a vocabulary index to a $d$-dimensional vector. The transformer encoder consumes the full patient history as the ordered sequence $(\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_L)$.

\paragraph{Sequence length and aggregation.}
The encoder processes a maximum of 512 positions per patient, a fixed architectural limit~\citep{Devlin2019BERT}. The first position is reserved for a special \texttt{CLS} token prepended to every sequence; for patients with longer histories, the oldest events are truncated and only the most recent 511 events are retained, on the assumption that recent clinical history is most predictive of near-term outcomes. The \texttt{CLS} token has no clinical meaning. It functions as an aggregation point: by the final transformer layer, the vector at the \texttt{CLS} position has attended to every other position in the sequence and serves as a fixed-length summary of the entire patient history~\citep{Devlin2019BERT}. This vector is the input to the classification head.

\subsubsection{XGBoost and Feedforward Neural Network: One-Hot Feature Vectors}

For the gradient-boosted ensemble (XGBoost) and the feedforward neural network, each patient's clinical history was represented as a sparse binary feature vector; chronological information was not preserved.

The feature space consisted of $K = 3,304$ unique PhecodeX phenotypes observed in the MarketScan training cohort. For each patient, the element $k$ of the vector was set to 1 if the phenotype $k$ appeared in the patient's record and 0 otherwise. To prevent outcome leakage, only phenotype codes recorded strictly before $t_{\mathrm{HF}}$ were included for case patients (groups~1--3); for control patients, no temporal restriction was applied. The two phenotypes of hip-fracture (MS \texttt{\_745.11} and \texttt{MS\_745.9}) were removed from the feature vocabulary of all patients. The resulting design matrix contained 12,174,785 rows and 3,304 columns, with a density of approximately 1.22\%, and was stored in Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) format for memory efficiency.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model Architectures and Training}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA}

BEHRT (BERT for Electronic Health Records)~\citep{Li2020BEHRT} is a transformer encoder adapted from the original BERT architecture~\citep{Devlin2019BERT,Vaswani2017Attention} for EHR data. Our implementation was first pre-trained on the full MarketScan corpus using a masked language-modeling (MLM) objective, and subsequently fine-tuned for four-class hip-fracture risk prediction.

\paragraph{Encoder architecture.}
The encoder follows the standard BERT design: a stack of transformer blocks, each comprising multi-head self-attention followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, with residual connections and layer normalization at each sub-layer~\citep{Vaswani2017Attention}. After the final transformer layer, the vector at the \texttt{CLS} position is passed to a linear classification head that produces logits over the four risk classes.

\paragraph{Architecture hyperparameter optimization.}
Architecture hyperparameters were selected by Bayesian optimization (Optuna~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna}, Tree-structured Parzen Estimator sampler, 30 trials), trained in a subset of 100{,}000 patients for five epochs per trial. The search space comprised: hidden dimension $d \in \{128, 192, 256, 288, 384\}$; number of layers in $\{2,\dots,8\}$; attention heads in $\{4, 6, 8, 12\}$; feed-forward intermediate dimension in $\{256, 512, 768, 1{,}024\}$; dropout in $[0.05, 0.30]$; and learning rate in $[10^{-5}, 5\times 10^{-4}]$.

\paragraph{Pre-training.}
The Pre-training used the MLM objective~\citep{Devlin2019BERT}: at each step, 15\% of the tokens in each sequence were randomly selected; of these, 80\% were replaced with \texttt{[MASK]}, 10\% with a random token drawn from the vocabulary, and 10\% were left unchanged. In all three cases the model was trained to predict the original token. This mixed corruption scheme prevents the model from learning representations that depend on the presence of the \texttt{[MASK]} symbol, which would otherwise degrade fine-tuning, where no masking is applied. Formally, given a sequence $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_L)$, a corrupted sequence $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$, and the set of masked positions $\mathcal{M}$, the pre-training loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MLM}} \;=\; -\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log P_\theta\!\left(x_i \,\big|\, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}\right),
\label{eq:mlm}
\end{equation}
where $P_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ is the predicted distribution of the model over the phenotype vocabulary at position $i$. Pre-training used a 90/10 train/validation split, up to 100 epochs, and early stopping with patience of 10 evaluations (4 evaluations per epoch). Training was distributed across four GPUs with an effective batch size of 1{,}024.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning with LoRA.}
After pre-training, the MLM prediction head was discarded and the BERT backbone was frozen. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\citep{Hu2022LoRA} was injected into the query and value projection matrices of every layer of self-attention. LoRA replaces a frozen weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ with parameterization
\begin{equation}
W \;=\; W_0 + \Delta W \;=\; W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,B A,
\qquad
A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d},\ \ B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\ \ r \ll d,
\label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is initialized from a Gaussian and $B$ is initialized to zero, so that $\Delta W = 0$ at the start of fine-tuning and the model output is unchanged from the pre-trained baseline. Only $A$ and $B$ are updated during fine-tuning; $W_0$ remains frozen. Because $r \ll d$, the number of trainable parameters per adapted layer drops from $d^2$ to $2rd$, a substantial reduction. We used $r = 16$, $\alpha = 32$ (effective scale $\alpha/r = 2$), and a LoRA dropout of 0.1, which left approximately 1--2\% of the total model parameters trainable. A linear classification head was appended to the frozen backbone and trained jointly with the LoRA adapters.

Fine-tuning used AdamW~\citep{Loshchilov2019AdamW} with learning rate $2\times 10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01, linear warmup over the first 10\% of training steps and gradient accumulation over 4 steps. Class imbalance was addressed via a class-weighted cross-entropy loss with weights inversely proportional to class frequency:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} \;=\; -\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} w_{y_n}\, \log \hat{p}_{n,y_n},
\qquad
w_c \;\propto\; \frac{1}{N_c},\quad \sum_{c=0}^{C-1} w_c = C,
\label{eq:weighted-ce}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}_{n,c}$ is the predicted probability that patient $n$ belongs to class $c$, $y_n \in \{0,\dots,C{-}1\}$ is the true label, $N_c$ is the number of training patients in class $c$, and $C = 4$. Training ran for up to 50 epochs, with early stopping (patience 10 epochs) based on validation loss.

\subsubsection{XGBoost}

A gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble was trained using XGBoost~\citep{Chen2016XGBoost}. XGBoost builds trees sequentially, with each new tree fitted to the gradient (and Hessian) of the loss with respect to the current ensemble's predictions; the final prediction is the sum of all trees' outputs passed through a softmax for multi-class classification.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
The training cohort contained approximately 99\% controls and $<\!1\%$ patients in the three fracture classes combined. To prevent the model from collapsing to the majority class, we (i)~down-sampled the control class to 600{,}000 patients (from over 10 million) and (ii)~applied the class weighting scheme of Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-ce} during training. Validation and test sets retained their natural class distributions to preserve unbiased estimates of generalization performance.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter optimization.}
Hyperparameters were tuned with Optuna~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna} (TPE sampler, 60 trials, 4 trials in parallel across 4 GPUs) using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the down-sampled training set; the objective was mean cross-entropy loss across folds. The selected configuration was: maximum tree depth~8; learning rate~0.282; row subsampling ratio~0.924; column subsampling ratio~0.666; minimum child weight~11; $\gamma = 1.138$; L1 regularization $\alpha = 2 \times 10^{-6}$; L2 regularization $\lambda = 2.256$; and 64 histogram bins. The final model was trained on the full down-sampled training set with these settings for up to 500 boosting rounds, with early stopping (patience 50 rounds) on the held-out validation set.

\subsubsection{Feedforward Neural Network}

A feedforward neural network (\texttt{SparseClassifier}) was trained on the same sparse one-hot feature matrix as XGBoost. The architecture takes a 3{,}304-dimensional input and passes it through three fully connected hidden layers of dimensions 512, 256, and 128, each followed by batch normalization, a ReLU activation, and dropout. A final linear layer maps the 128-dimensional hidden representation to four class logits, from which class probabilities are obtained via the softmax function.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
We used the same strategy as for XGBoost: the control class was sampled down to 600{,}000 patients, and a class-weighted cross-entropy loss (Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-ce}) was applied during training.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter optimization.}
Hyperparameters were selected with Optuna (TPE sampler, 30 trials)~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna} in a stratified subset of 150{,}000 training patients, using a split 80/20 train/validation per trial and early stopping after four epochs without improvement. The tuned parameters were learning rate, weight decay, dropout rate, and batch size. The selected configuration was: learning rate $2.98 \times 10^{-4}$; weight decay $9.59 \times 10^{-4}$; dropout 0.306; batch size 1{,}024. The final model was trained on the complete down-sampled training set with these settings for up to 60 epochs, using AdamW~\citep{Loshchilov2019AdamW} with a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler (factor 0.5, patience 3 epochs) and early stopping on cross-entropy validation loss (patience 10 epochs).

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{External Validation in All of Us}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

All three models trained in MarketScan were applied without modification to the All of Us validation cohort. The model weights were not updated during validation. The cohort and feature construction followed the same pipelines used for MarketScan, with the adaptations described below.

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA}

The diagnosis sequences were constructed using the same procedure as for MarketScan. The ICD-to-PhecodeX mapping was executed in BigQuery on approximately 123 million condition records. To ensure that phenotype tokens mapped to the same embedding vectors as during pre-training, all All of Us sequences were tokenized using the MarketScan-derived vocabulary; phenotypes present in All of Us but absent from the MarketScan vocabulary were mapped to the \texttt{UNK} token.

Because transformer inference is substantially more expensive than scoring sparse feature vectors, BEHRT-LoRA was evaluated in a random subsample stratified by class of $N = 50{,}000$ patients. Stratified sampling preserved within-class proportions, so AUROC, AUPRC, and per-class metrics remain directly comparable with those obtained from XGBoost and the neural network, which were evaluated in the full cohort of 98{,}249 patients.

\subsubsection{XGBoost and Feedforward Neural Network}

A sparse binary feature matrix was constructed using the same procedure as for MarketScan, restricted to the same 3{,}304 PhecodeX columns used at training. The 146 PhecodeX phenotypes that appeared in All of Us but were absent from the MarketScan feature set were discarded; columns observed in MarketScan but not in All of Us were retained as all-zero vectors to preserve input dimensionality.

\section{Results}

We evaluated BEHRT-LoRA, XGBoost, and a feedforward neural network (NN) on the MarketScan held-out test set. Due to class imbalance (the control class accounts for approximately 99\% of the cohort), ROC-AUC and AUPRC were treated as the primary 
measures of predictive ability. BEHRT-LoRA was evaluated on a class-stratified subsample of $N=50{,}000$ patients; XGBoost and NN were evaluated on the full test set ($N=1{,}217{,}479$). Confidence intervals were obtained by patient-level resampling ($N=1{,}000$ iterations for BEHRT-LoRA; $N=500$ for XGBoost and NN).

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Overall Discrimination}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Unlike overall accuracy, which is dominated by the most prevalent group, macro-averaged metrics weight each risk group equally by 
computing performance separately per group and averaging the results regardless of group size. Because the three fracture risk 
groups are rare but carry the greatest clinical significance, macro-averaged metrics were used as the summary measure of model performance.


BEHRT-LoRA achieved the highest macro-averaged performance on the 
held-out test set, outperforming XGBoost and the neural network on 
every summary metric (Table~\ref{tab:overall_comparison}). The 
macro-averaged ROC-AUC was 0.978 for BEHRT-LoRA, compared to 0.764 
for XGBoost and 0.783 for the neural network. In practical terms, 
BEHRT-LoRA correctly ranked a high-risk patient above a low-risk 
patient in roughly 98 out of 100 comparisons, while the simpler 
models did so in roughly 78 out of 100.

The ROC curves, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:roc_curves}, capture how 
well each model separates risk groups across all possible decision thresholds, but they can be overly optimistic when cases are rare. The precision-recall curves in Figure~\ref{fig:pr_curves} tell a 
more demanding story: they show the trade-off between how many true fracture cases the model identifies and how often it raises 
a false alarm. A model that flags every patient as high-risk will find all true cases but will be wrong almost every time, given how rare fractures are in this cohort. The area under the precision-recall curve therefore reflects whether a model can 
identify a meaningful proportion of at-risk patients without overwhelming clinicians with false positives, which is the 
practically relevant question in a screening setting.

\subsection{Per-Class Classification Performance}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The performance advantage of BEHRT-LoRA was consistent across all four risk groups and was most pronounced among the three fracture risk groups, each accounting for less than 1\% of the cohort. Per-class ROC-AUC 
exceeded 0.96 in all four groups for BEHRT-LoRA, with lower confidence interval bounds remaining above 0.957, indicating stable and reliable separation between risk groups. XGBoost and the neural network achieved per-class ROC-AUC between 0.73 and 0.87, a range that in a screening context would translate to a substantial number of high-risk patients being unidentified (Table~\ref{tab:per_class_discrimination}).

The precision-recall curves revealed an even sharper contrast. For the three fracture risk groups, BEHRT-LoRA achieved an area 
under the precision-recall curve between 0.62 and 0.63, meaning that even among a population where true fracture cases are rare, the model retained meaningful ability to identify them without excessive false alarms. XGBoost and the neural network achieved values between 0.015 and 0.018 on the same groups, barely above the baseline expected from a model that assigns risk at random.

\subsection{Sensitivity, PPV, and NPV}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

To assess performance at a fixed decision rule, each patient was assigned to the risk group the model considered most likely, and 
sensitivity and positive predictive value were computed from the resulting assignments.
BEHRT-LoRA identified between 77\% and 82\% of patients in each fracture risk group, with positive predictive values between 12\% 
and 24\%, meaning that between one in eight and one in four patients flagged by the model had a true fracture in that time 
window (Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic_comparison}). XGBoost and the neural network reached comparable sensitivity in some risk groups, 
but did so with positive predictive values of 1\% to 2\%, meaning that fewer than one in fifty patients flagged as high-risk 
actually belonged to that group. Negative predictive values exceeded 99.8\% across all three models and all fracture risk 
groups, a figure that reflects the rarity of the outcome rather than model sensitivity.

The confusion matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices} 
provide a visual account of where each model places its predictions. For BEHRT-LoRA, predictions are concentrated along 
the diagonal, indicating that patients are most often assigned to their true risk group. For XGBoost and the neural network, 
predictions for the fracture risk groups are more widely distributed across other classes, particularly for the three 
fracture timing groups.

\subsection{Probability Calibration}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Calibration describes how well a model's predicted probabilities reflect observed event rates in practice. The Brier score measures the average squared difference between predicted probabilities and true outcomes. Lower values of Brier Score indicate better calibration and a score equal to the class prevalence represents the performance of a model that ignores all patient information and simply predicts the average rate.
In this case, all three models were poorly calibrated. The discrepancy between predicted probabilities and observed 
outcomes was largest for XGBoost and the neural network on the control class, with raw Brier scores of 0.30 and 0.38 
respectively, against a prevalence-only baseline of 0.010. This indicated that their raw probability outputs were substantially 
less informative than simply predicting the proportion of control patients in the cohort. The reliability diagrams in 
Figure~\ref{fig:calibration_raw} illustrate this directly: a perfectly calibrated model would follow the diagonal line, where 
predicted probability equals observed frequency. Before calibration, XGBoost and the neural network deviate substantially 
from this line, while BEHRT-LoRA tracks closer. However, BEHRT-LoRA shows overconfidence at intermediate predicted probabilities, assigning higher probabilities than the observed event rates justify.

The models were fit using isotonic regression 70\% of the evaluation set. All three models moved toward the diagonal and Brier scores dropped sharply 
(Table~\ref{tab:brier_calibration_comparison}). BEHRT-LoRA retained the lowest Brier score in every risk group after 
calibration. For XGBoost and the neural network, Brier scores after calibration converged to values near the prevalence-only 
baseline, indicating that recalibration corrected the shape of their probability outputs but did not recover probabilistic 
information that was absent from the raw predictions.
