\section{Methods}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Design and Overview}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

In this retrospective cohort study, we used the IBM MarketScan Commercial Claims Database to develop and validate three machine learning models to predict hip fracture risk in patients with diabetes. The prediction task is framed as four-class classification, stratifying patients by the time from first recorded diabetes diagnosis to first recorded hip fracture.
Three models were evaluated: (1) BEHRT-LoRA, a transformer encoder adapted from BERT for electronic health records, pre-trained on the full MarketScan corpus and fine-tuned for classification using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA); 
(2) XGBoost, a gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble; and (3) a feedforward neural network (\texttt{SparseClassifier}). Trained models were subsequently applied without re-training to an
independent external validation cohort drawn from the All of Us Research
Program. This allowed us to assess generalization across diverse populations.


%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data Sources}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{IBM MarketScan Commercial Claims Database}

%% TODO: Insert years of data, enrollment figures, and any
%%       relevant database version information here.
MarketScan contains de-identified administrative claims from 
employer-sponsored health plans across the United States, covering patients enrolled between January 2003 and January 2024. Data elements used in this study include inpatient and outpatient diagnosis codes (ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM), continuous enrollment 
records, and patient demographic information.

\subsubsection{All of Us Research Program}

The \textit{All of Us} Research Program is a longitudinal prospective cohort
funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), enrolling U.S.\ residents
aged 18 years or older from more than 340 recruitment sites nationwide since
May 2018~\cite{AllofUs_NEJM}.
The program explicitly targets groups historically underrepresented in
biomedical research, including racial and ethnic minorities, individuals with
limited healthcare access, and other sociodemographically diverse
populations~\cite{AllofUs_diversity}.

Participant data are organized into a Curated Data Repository (CDR) with
three access tiers: Public, Registered, and Controlled.
This study used the Controlled Tier CDR~v8
(dataset version \texttt{C2024Q3R8}), accessed via the All of Us
Researcher Workbench, which includes data from participants enrolled
through October~1, 2023.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Cohort and Inclusion Criteria}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The MarketScan study cohort was constructed by linking three administrative tables and retaining only patients meeting all criteria simultaneously.

\textbf{Enrollment table.} Patients were required to have at least one full year of continuous insurance coverage, identifying 138{,}879{,}229 eligible enrollment records.

\textbf{Demographics table.} Only patients aged 20 years or older at the start of their first enrollment interval were retained, identifying 179{,}415{,}164 eligible demographic records.

\textbf{Diagnosis table.} Patients were required to have at least one claim mapped to a diabetes-specific PhecodeX code: 
\texttt{EM\_202} (type~2 diabetes mellitus), \texttt{EM\_202.1}, 
\texttt{EM\_202.2}, or \texttt{BI\_181} (type~1 diabetes mellitus), 
identifying 18{,}110{,}999 patients with a qualifying diagnosis.

The inner join of all three tables yielded the final study cohort 
of $N = 12{,}174{,}785$ patients meeting all criteria.

\paragraph{Outcome definition and risk-group assignment.}

As diabetes diagnoses accumulate over time, the index date was defined as the date of each patient's earliest recorded diabetes diagnosis.
PhecodeX codes \texttt{MS\_745.11} (fracture of femur) and \texttt{MS\_745.9} (pathological fracture) were used to identify patients with a hip fracture and the earliest hip fracture date was assigned as the outcome index date for each patient.

We then split the target cohort into four mutually exclusive risk groups based on the number of days between the diabetes index date and the outcome index date. Patients with no fracture, a fracture predating the diabetes diagnosis, or a fracture more than ten years after the index date were consolidated into a single reference class, as hip fracture 
is a rare event and a gap exceeding ten years makes a causal relationship with diabetes unlikely.

\subsection{All of Us Validation Cohort}

The diabetes diagnosis and age criteria were applied to the All of Us CDR~v8 dataset (\texttt{C2024Q3R8}), yielding a validation cohort of 98{,}399 participants with a documented diabetes diagnosis and at least one EHR condition record. Continuous enrollment was not required, as All of Us is a prospective research cohort rather than an insurance claims database. 
Analyses were conducted within the Researcher Workbench using BigQuery for cohort construction.

\subsection{Feature Representation}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{BEHRT: EHR Token Sequences}

\paragraph{Code representation.}
We started with raw ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes from the MarketScan claims and mapped them to PhecodeX phenotypes. This mapping groups semantically related ICD codes into a single clinical phenotype label, reducing vocabulary size and improving generalization across code versions.

\paragraph{Sequence construction.}
For each patient in the study cohort, all diagnosis events were sorted chronologically and restricted to those occurring before the hip fracture outcome index date. Hip fracture phenotypes (\texttt{MS\_745.11} and \texttt{MS\_745.9}) were excluded entirely to prevent outcome leakage in both control and fracture risk groups. Events occurring on the same calendar day were grouped into a single visit; a separator marker (\texttt{SEP}) was placed between 
consecutive visits, and a final \texttt{SEP} was appended at the end of the record. Age at each event was computed by subtracting the patient's birth year from the calendar year of the event. Because sex does not vary over time, a single sex value was assigned uniformly across all positions in the sequence.

\paragraph{Model input format.}

Each input value in a patient's history --- a diagnosis, an age at the time of diagnosis or a sex label --- is referred to as a \textit{token}. Unlike a text corpus, which contains only word tokens, a medical record contains several distinct token types. All tokens are assembled into \textit{vocabularies} as shown in Table~\ref{tab:vocabularies}.

The transformer cannot process tokens in their raw form. It requires every input to be expressed as a fixed-length vector of real numbers. For every token in each vocabulary, the model therefore maintains one such vector, called an \textit{embedding}. When the 
model encounters a token, it retrieves the corresponding embedding vector. These vectors are not 
predefined; they are initialized randomly and adjusted during training so that tokens with similar clinical meaning converge to similar numeric representations.

Two additional embeddings encode structural rather than clinical information. A \textit{segment embedding} tells the model which visit each diagnosis belongs to: its value alternates between distinct visits, so all diagnoses within the same visit share the 
same segment value. A \textit{position embedding} tells the model the order of visits: it increments once per visit rather than once per diagnosis, so the model tracks the sequence of visits rather than the 
sequence of individual events.

At each position in the sequence, all five embedding vectors --- phenotype, age, sex, segment, and position --- are summed into a single combined vector. The transformer then receives the full patient history as an ordered sequence of these combined per-event vectors.
\paragraph{Sequence length and aggregation.}
The transformer can only process 512 positions at once, a fixed architectural limit.
When a patient's sequence exceeds that, the oldest events are dropped and only the most recent 511 are kept, on the assumption that recent clinical history is most predictive.
One position is always reserved for a special \texttt{CLS} token prepended to the front of every sequence, hence 511 rather than 512.
The \texttt{CLS} token has no clinical meaning; it acts as a collection point that the transformer uses to aggregate information from the entire sequence.
By the time the sequence exits the transformer, the vector at the \texttt{CLS} position has attended to every other position and effectively summarizes the whole patient history.

\subsubsection{XGBoost and Neural Network: One-Hot Encoded Feature Vectors}

For the gradient-boosted decision tree (XGBoost) and feedforward neural network models, each patient's clinical history was represented as a binary feature vector. No chronological information was preserved.

The feature space consisted of $N=$3{,}304 unique PhecodeX phenotypes observed across the MarketScan training cohort. 
Each element in the vector served as a binary indicator, set to 1 if the corresponding phenotype was present in the patient's clinical record and 0 otherwise. 
To prevent data leakage during learning, for patients in target groups, only phenotype codes recorded strictly before the date of the outcome index were included.
For patients in the control group, no temporal restriction was applied.
Furthermore, the target hip fracture phenotypes (\texttt{MS\_745.11} and \texttt{MS\_745.9}) were explicitly removed from the feature vocabulary for all patients.
The resulting dataset comprised $N=$12{,}174{,}785 patient vectors. Given the sparsity of clinical claims data (matrix density approximately 1.22\%), the feature matrix was stored in Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) format.

\subsection{Model Architectures}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA}

BEHRT (BERT for Electronic Health Records)~\cite{TODO_BEHRT} is a
transformer encoder adapted from the original BERT architecture for medical claims data.
The model was first pre-trained on the full MarketScan patient corpususing a masked language modelling (MLM) objective, and subsequently fine-tuned for four-class hip fracture risk prediction.

\paragraph{Encoder architecture.}
The encoder follows the standard BERT design: a stack of transformer layers, each comprising multi-head self-attention followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, with residual connections and layer normalisation at each sub-layer. After the final transformer layer, the vector at the \texttt{CLS} position serves as a patient-level summary and is passed to the classification head to produce risk class probabilities.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter optimization.}
Architecture hyperparameters were selected using Bayesian optimisation (Optuna, TPE sampler, 30 trials) on a subset of 
100{,}000 training patients over 5 epochs per trial. The search 
ranges were: hidden dimension $\in \{128, 192, 256, 288, 384\}$; 
number of layers 2--8; attention heads $\in \{4, 6, 8, 12\}$; 
feed-forward intermediate dimension $\in \{256, 512, 768, 1{,}024\}$; 
dropout 0.05--0.30; learning rate $10^{-5}$--$5 \times 10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Pre-training.}

The model was pre-trained on the MarketScan training corpus using the masked language modelling (MLM) objective. At each training step, 15\% of tokens in each sequence were randomly selected for prediction. Of these, 80\% were replaced with a \texttt{[MASK]} token, 10\% were replaced with a randomly drawn token from the 
vocabulary, and 10\% were left unchanged. In all three cases, the model was trained to predict the original token. This mixed corruption strategy prevents the model from learning to attend only to masked positions, which would reduce the quality of representations at fine-tuning time when no masking is applied.
Training used a 90/10 train/validation split, with up to 100 epochs and early stopping (patience 10 evaluations, with 4 evaluations per epoch). Training was distributed across 4 GPUs with an effective batch size of 1{,}024.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning with LoRA.}
Following pre-training, the MLM prediction head was discarded and the BERT backbone was frozen.
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{TODO_LoRA} was injected into the query and value projection matrices of every attention layer.
LoRA replaces each target weight matrix $W$ with $W + BA$, where $B$ and $A$ are low-rank factors ($A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$) initialized so that $BA = 0$ at the start of fine-tuning.
Only $A$ and $B$ are updated during fine-tuning; the original $W$ is frozen.
We used rank $r = 16$, scaling factor $\alpha = 32$ (effective scale $\alpha / r = 2.0$), and LoRA dropout of 0.1, resulting in approximately 1--2\% of total parameters being trainable.
A linear classification head was appended to the frozen backbone and also trained.
Fine-tuning used AdamW ($\text{lr} = 2 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01) with a linear warmup over 10\% of training steps, gradient accumulation over 4 steps, and a class-weighted cross-entropy loss
with weights inversely proportional to class frequency.
Training ran for up to 50 epochs with early stopping (patience 10 epochs) on the held-out validation loss.

\subsubsection{XGBoost}

A gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble was trained using XGBoost~\cite{TODO_XGBoost} on the sparse one-hot binary feature matrix. XGBoost builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, where each
new tree is trained to correct the errors of the trees already in the ensemble; the final prediction is the weighted sum of all trees'
outputs.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
The training set contains approximately 99\% control patients and $<$1\% fracture cases across the three risk classes.
To prevent the model from ignoring minority classes, the training set was downsampled so that the control class was capped at 600{,}000 patients (from over 10 million), and sample weights inversely proportional to class frequency were applied during training. The validation and test sets were left at their natural class distributions.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter optimization.}
Hyperparameters were selected using Optuna Bayesian optimisation (TPE sampler, 60 trials, 4 trials in parallel across 4 GPUs) with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the downsampled training set.
The objective minimised mean cross-entropy loss across folds. The best hyperparameter configuration found was:
maximum tree depth~8; learning rate~0.282; subsampling ratio~0.924;
column subsampling ratio~0.666; minimum child weight~11;
$\gamma = 1.138$; $\alpha = 2 \times 10^{-6}$;
$\lambda = 2.256$; histogram bin count~64.
The final model was trained on the full downsampled training set
using these parameters for 500 boosting rounds with early stopping
on the validation set (patience~50 rounds).

\subsubsection{Feedforward Neural Network}

A feedforward neural network (\texttt{SparseClassifier}) was trained on the same sparse one-hot feature matrix as XGBoost. The architecture takes a 3{,}304-dimensional sparse input and passes it through three fully connected hidden layers of dimensions 512, 256, and 128, each followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, and dropout. The final linear layer maps the 128-dimensional hidden representation to four class logits, from which class probabilities are obtained via softmax.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
The same downsampling strategy as XGBoost was applied: the control class was capped at 600{,}000 training patients, and a class-weighted cross-entropy loss was used, with weights inversely proportional to class frequency in the training set.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter optimization.}
Hyperparameters were selected using Optuna (TPE sampler, 30 trials) on a stratified subset of 150{,}000 training patients, with an 80/20
train/validation split per trial and early stopping after 4 epochs without improvement.
The tuned parameters were learning rate, weight decay, dropout rate, and batch size.
The best configuration was: learning rate~$2.98 \times 10^{-4}$; weight decay~$9.59 \times 10^{-4}$; dropout~0.306; batch size~1{,}024.
The final model was trained on the full downsampled training set using these hyperparameters for up to 60 epochs with early stopping (patience~10 epochs) based on validation cross-entropy loss.
The AdamW optimiser was used with a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau}
learning rate scheduler (factor~0.5, patience~3 epochs).



%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{External Validation on All of Us}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

All three models trained on MarketScan were applied without modification to the All of Us validation cohort described. No model weights were updated during 
validation. Data construction followed the same pipeline as MarketScan with adaptations disclosed below. 

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA}

Diagnosis sequences were constructed following the same procedure as MarketScan. ICD-to-PhecodeX mapping was executed in BigQuery over 123~million condition rows. To ensure that phenotype tokens mapped to the same embedding vectors as during MarketScan training, all All of Us sequences were encoded using the MarketScan-derived vocabulary. 
PhecodeX phenotypes present in All of Us but absent from the MarketScan vocabulary were mapped to the \texttt{UNK} token.

Because transformer inference is substantially more expensive than scoring sparse feature vectors, BEHRT-LoRA was evaluated on a class-stratified random subsample of $N=50{,}000$ patients. 
Stratified sampling preserved within-class proportions, keeping AUROC, AUPRC, and per-class metrics directly comparable with XGBoost and the neural network, which were evaluated on the full cohort of 98{,}249 patients.

\subsubsection{XGBoost and Neural Network}

A sparse binary feature matrix was constructed following the same procedure as MarketScan, restricted to the same 3{,}304 phecode columns used during training. The 146 phecodes present in All of Us but absent from the MarketScan feature set were discarded; columns with no All of Us representation were retained as all-zero vectors to preserve input dimensionality.
