\begin{abstract}
Patients with diabetes face a two to five times increased risk of hip
fracture, yet the standard clinical instrument, FRAX, requires a patient
interview and underestimates the risk in this population. We developed a
transformer-based model (BEHRT-LoRA) that predicts hip fracture from
routinely coded electronic health records alone, representing each
diagnosis as a learned embedding so that the model can exploit
similarities between related conditions rather than treating codes as
independent features. The model was trained in 12{,}033{,}245 patients
with diabetes in the Merative MarketScan database, stratified into four
risk classes by time of fracture and compared to XGBoost and a
feedforward neural network. BEHRT-LoRA achieved a macro-averaged AUROC
of 0.978, compared with 0.761 (XGBoost) and 0.741 (NN). In an
independent cohort of patients with diabetes from the University of Chicago with clinically
assigned FRAX scores, BEHRT-LoRA outperformed FRAX on both AUROC
(0.884 vs.\ 0.765) and AUPRC (0.125 vs.\ 0.024). The findings were
confirmed in external validation using the \textit{All of Us} Research
Program.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Diabetes is among the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, affecting an estimated 589~million adults aged 20--79 years in 2024, with projections reaching 853~million by 2050~\citep{IDF2025Atlas,Genitsaridi2026Lancet}. In addition to well-recognized microvascular complications such as retinopathy and neuropathy, diabetes confers a substantially increased risk of fragility (low-trauma) fracture. Patients with diabetes have a 2- to 5-fold higher risk of hip fracture than those without diabetes~\citep{Schwartz2001JCEM,Vestergaard2005Diabetologia,Giangregorio2012JBMR,Sellmeyer2016Diabetes,Janghorbani2006DiabetesCare}, and after long-term follow-up, approximately half of all patients with diabetes will sustain a fracture~\citep{Melton2008JBMR}. The excess risk is multifactorial, reflecting higher falls rates, reduced bone turnover, and altered properties of bone material~\citep{Pijpers2012AgeAgeing,Hygum2017EJE,Furst2016JCEM}.

Despite this elevated risk, identifying which patients with diabetes will fracture remains difficult, and many are never diagnosed with elevated fracture risk. The Fracture Risk Assessment Tool (FRAX) is the most widely used prediction instrument and combines demographic, anthropometric, and clinical risk factors to estimate the 10-year probability of fracture~\citep{Kanis2010FRAX}. However, FRAX requires inputs typically obtained through direct patient interview, which demands clinician time, and in practice it is most often calculated only after bone mineral density testing. Moreover, FRAX has demonstrated only moderate discriminatory ability for hip fracture (with, the area under the receiver operating characteristic, AUROC, $\approx 0.78$) and has repeatedly been shown to underestimate hip fracture risk in patients with diabetes~\citep{Giangregorio2012JBMR,Beaudoin2019OsteoInt,Jain2025JBMR}.

Diabetes is a heterogeneous disease whose fracture risk factors may differ from those operative in the general population, suggesting that disease-specific prediction tools are warranted. Advances in predictive modeling could enable targeted bone-health screening for patients with diabetes without requiring additional clinician time or bone mineral density testing. To date, however, only a small number of studies have examined machine-learning approaches to fracture prediction in this population~\citep{Li2023JOSR}. The present study was therefore undertaken to develop and validate machine-learning models for the prediction of hip fracture in patients with diabetes using a large, heterogeneous, and generalizable data source. Specifically, we trained models on the Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims Database and externally validated them using data from the National Institutes of Health \textit{All of Us} Research Program.

\section{Results}

To give the reader a clinical context regarding predictive pathologies, in Figure \ref{fig:fractures} we show the diseases that were most predictive of future fractures.

We evaluated BEHRT-LoRA, XGBoost, and a feedforward neural network (NN) on the MarketScan held-out test set. Due to class imbalance, with the control class accounting for approximately 99\% of the cohort, ROC-AUC and AUPRC were treated as the primary measures of predictive performance. All three models were evaluated on the full held-out test set ($N=1{,}203{,}325$ for XGBoost and NN; $N=1{,}203{,}053$ for BEHRT-LoRA, the small difference reflecting BEHRT's per-patient maximum sequence-length filter), stratified 10\% split with seed~35, using identical patient identifiers for the XGBoost/NN pair to permit direct comparison. Confidence intervals were obtained by patient-level resampling ($N=200$ iterations, shared random-number generator across models).

\subsection{Overall Discrimination Among Fracture Classes}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

In contrast to overall accuracy, which is dominated by the most prevalent class, macro-averaged metrics weight each risk group equally by computing performance separately within each group and averaging across groups regardless of size. Because the three fracture risk groups are rare but carry the greatest clinical significance, macro-averaged metrics were adopted as the principal summary of model performance.

BEHRT-LoRA achieved the highest macro-averaged performance on the held-out test set, outperforming XGBoost and the neural network on every summary metric (Table~\ref{tab:overall_comparison}). The macro-averaged ROC-AUC was 0.978 for BEHRT-LoRA, compared to 0.761 for XGBoost and 0.741 for the neural network. In practical terms, BEHRT-LoRA correctly ranked a high-risk patient above a low-risk patient in approximately 98 out of 100 comparisons, while the simpler models did so in approximately 76 and 74 out of 100, respectively.

The ROC curves shown in Figure~\ref{fig:roc_panel}\textbf{a}--\textbf{c} characterize how well each model separates risk groups across all possible decision thresholds; however, ROC analysis can be overly optimistic when cases are rare. The precision--recall curves in the MarketScan column of Figure~\ref{fig:pr_panel} (panels \textbf{a}, \textbf{c}, \textbf{e}) provide a more demanding evaluation: they depict the trade-off between the proportion of true fracture cases identified and the rate at which false alarms are raised. A model that flags every patient as high-risk would identify all true cases but would be incorrect nearly every time, given the rarity of fractures in this cohort. The area under the precision--recall curve therefore reflects whether a model can identify a meaningful proportion of at-risk patients without overwhelming clinicians with false positives, which is the practically relevant question in a screening setting.

All three models discriminated above chance on the external cohort, but the magnitude of the drop relative to the MarketScan held-out test set differed sharply across architectures. The ROC and precision--recall curves in Figures~\ref{fig:roc_panel}\textbf{d}--\textbf{f} and~\ref{fig:pr_panel}\textbf{d}--\textbf{f} show this attenuation directly: for all three models the curves remain visibly separated from the diagonal across all classes, but the per-class spread is wider and the AUPRC values are lower than on MarketScan, reflecting both the lower fracture prevalence in All of Us and a genuine covariate shift between the two populations.

\subsection{Per-Class Classification Performance}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The performance advantage of BEHRT-LoRA was consistent between all four risk groups and was more pronounced for the three fracture risk groups, each of which represents less than 1\% of the cohort. The per-class ROC-AUC was at least 0.97 in all four BEHRT-LoRA groups, with lower confidence interval bounds remaining above 0.96, indicating a stable and reliable separation between risk groups. XGBoost and the neural network achieved per-class ROC-AUC between 0.69 and 0.82, a range that, in a screening context, would translate into a substantial number of high-risk patients that remain unidentified (Table~\ref{tab:per_class_discrimination_alt2}).

The precision--recall curves revealed an even sharper contrast. For the three fracture risk groups, BEHRT-LoRA achieved an area under the precision recall curve between 0.47 and 0.67, indicating that, even in a population in which true fracture cases are rare, the model retained a meaningful ability to identify them without excessive false alarms. XGBoost and the neural network achieved values between 0.011 and 0.017 in the same groups, barely above the baseline expected from a model that assigns risk at random.

\subsection{Sensitivity, PPV, and NPV}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

To assess performance at a fixed decision rule, each patient was assigned to the risk group the model considered the most likely, and sensitivity and positive predictive value were calculated from the resulting assignments. This argmax assignment defines the intended screening operating point: a patient placed in any of the three fracture classes is flagged for formal fracture-risk assessment. At this operating point, BEHRT-LoRA identified between 69\% and 81\% of patients in each fracture risk group, with positive predictive values between 13\% and 26\%, indicating that between roughly one in eight and one in four patients flagged by the model had a true fracture within the corresponding time window (Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic_comparison}). XGBoost and the neural network achieved comparable sensitivity in some risk groups but with positive predictive values of 1\% to 2\%, indicating that fewer than one in fifty patients flagged as high-risk actually belonged to that group. Negative predictive values exceeded 99.6\% in all three models and all fracture risk groups, a figure that reflects the rarity of the outcome rather than the sensitivity of the model.

The confusion matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices}\textbf{a}--\textbf{c} provide a visual account of where each model places its predictions. For BEHRT-LoRA, the predictions are concentrated along the diagonal, indicating that patients are assigned more often to their true risk group. For XGBoost and the neural network, predictions for fracture risk groups are more widely distributed across other classes, particularly for the three fracture timing groups.

\subsubsection{External Validation (All of Us)}

The same operating-point analysis was repeated in the \textit{All of Us} cohort, where the sensitivity--PPV trade-off observed on MarketScan was attenuated by the much lower base rate of fracture (Table~\ref{tab:aou_diagnostic_comparison}). The three models adopted markedly different argmax strategies on the external data. BEHRT-LoRA assigned 94.7\% of all participants to the control class, achieving 94.9\% sensitivity on controls but identifying only 20.0\%, 14.6\%, and 10.9\% of $<$1-year, 1--5-year, and 5--10-year cases. XGBoost and the neural network distributed their predictions more broadly across the four classes, recovering higher sensitivity on the $<$1-year group (61.9\% for XGBoost, 64.4\% for NN) at the cost of low control-class sensitivity (48.5\% and 38.7\%, respectively). PPV for the three fracture classes was low across all three models because of the extreme class imbalance in the external cohort (99.08\% control). BEHRT-LoRA achieved the highest PPV in every fracture class (2.5\% for $<$1-year, 2.9\% for 1--5-year, 2.1\% for 5--10-year), corresponding to a six- to twelve-fold enrichment over the marginal prevalence in each group. The confusion matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices}\textbf{d}--\textbf{f} illustrate these distinct strategies. Negative predictive values exceeded 99.6\% in all three models and all fracture risk groups, but this reflects the rarity of the outcome rather than discriminative ability.

\subsection{Probability Calibration}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Calibration describes the agreement between a model's predicted probabilities and observed event rates. The Brier score, which is the average squared difference between predicted probabilities and true outcomes, was used as a summary calibration metric. Lower Brier scores indicate better calibration; a Brier score equal to the class prevalence corresponds to the performance of a model that ignores all patient information and predicts the average event rate.

All three models were poorly calibrated. The discrepancy between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was the greatest for XGBoost and the neural network in the control class, with raw Brier scores of 0.31 and 0.32, respectively, against a prevalence-only baseline of 0.010. This finding indicates that the raw probability outputs of these models were substantially less informative than simply predicting the proportion of control patients in the cohort. Before calibration, the raw Brier scores of XGBoost and the neural network departed far from the prevalence-only baseline, whereas those of BEHRT-LoRA stayed much closer to it. BEHRT-LoRA nonetheless over-predicted risk in the minority classes, while under-predicting the control class.

The models were re-calibrated using an isotonic regression fit in 70\% of the evaluation set. All three models' Brier scores decreased substantially after recalibration (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:brier_calibration_comparison}). BEHRT-LoRA retained the lowest Brier score in all risk groups after calibration. For XGBoost and the neural network, Brier scores after calibration converged toward values near the prevalence-only baseline, indicating that recalibration corrected the shape of their probability outputs, but did not recover probabilistic information absent from the raw predictions.

\subsubsection{External Validation (All of Us)}

Calibration was reassessed in the All of Us cohort using the same isotonic procedure applied to the MarketScan held-out test set. Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:aou_brier_calibration_comparison} reports raw and isotonic-calibrated Brier scores.

Raw Brier scores on the external cohort were higher than on MarketScan for every model and every class, but the ordering across models was preserved: BEHRT-LoRA had the lowest raw Brier score in each class, followed by XGBoost, then the neural network. After isotonic recalibration in the All of Us cohort, all three models converged to values essentially equal to the prevalence-only baseline. Because calibrated Brier scores match the baseline almost exactly, the isotonic step in this cohort effectively reduces the probability outputs to a near-constant prediction equal to the class prevalence, indicating that little additional probabilistic information could be recovered from the raw outputs beyond what the marginal class distribution already provides. These results indicate that the model outputs should be interpreted as a relative risk-ranking, or triage, signal rather than as calibrated absolute fracture probabilities; the deployment claims throughout are therefore framed in terms of how well the model separates and ranks patients by risk, not in terms of probability estimates.

\section{Discussion}

Our study demonstrates that advanced predictive modeling such as BEHRT-LoRA can identify patients with diabetes at elevated risk of hip fracture from the electronic health record alone, without requiring additional clinical effort or time. The model could be continuously applied across the entire population of patients with diabetes within a health system, identifying the small subset of individuals that warrant a formal fracture risk assessment without any additional clinical workload. This is particularly important for patients with diabetes, for whom multiple screening tests are already required (A1c measurements, lipid panels, eye exams, and foot exams, among others). Our model has a number of advantages over existing models: it is specific to diabetes, includes patients over a wide age range, does not require bone density testing, and was validated in \textit{All of Us}, a large cohort that included substantial numbers of racial and ethnic minority patients. To our knowledge, no previous fracture prediction model for diabetes has performed so well.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{How Clinicians Currently Assess Hip Fracture Risk}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The current standard for the assessment of fracture risk in clinical practice is FRAX, a web-based algorithm that estimates the 10-year probability of major osteoporotic or hip fracture from a small set of dichotomized risk factors: age, sex, body mass index, prior fracture, parental hip fracture, current smoking, glucocorticoid use, rheumatoid arthritis, secondary osteoporosis, and alcohol use~\citep{Kanis2010FRAX}. FRAX is typically applied after a clinical interview and, according to most guideline frameworks, is recommended in conjunction with bone mineral density (BMD) testing by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). These resources are not uniformly available and are rarely deployed in the absence of pre-existing clinical suspicion of elevated risk. DXA testing is recommended in the general population for women over 65 years of age; the American Diabetes Association recommends that all individuals with diabetes (both men and women) over 65 years of age, or over 50 years of age with risk factors, receive DXA testing. However, owing to the number of screening tests already required in patients with diabetes and the poor recognition of fracture risk in this population, BMD testing is often not ordered or performed, and fracture risk is therefore never assessed. In practice, FRAX is rarely calculated in patients with diabetes, unless the provider has specifically considered fragility fracture. Consequently, a large proportion of at-risk patients are never identified.

Another limitation is that FRAX was not designed for patients with diabetes and systematically underestimates the risk of fractures in this group. Because BMD is often preserved or even elevated in type~2 diabetes despite deficits in bone quality, BMD-based FRAX scores do not capture the excessive structural fragility characteristic of diabetic bone disease~\citep{Giangregorio2012JBMR,Beaudoin2019OsteoInt,Jain2025JBMR}. The tool also lacks variables for type of diabetes, duration of the disease, glycemic control, and exposure to medications, each of which modulates the risk of fractures. Therefore, even an optimally administered FRAX is an incomplete summary of risk in this population.

Other fracture prediction models exist but are not tailored to diabetes. QFracture includes diabetes as a risk factor but has not been specifically tested in patients with diabetes and was not calibrated for use outside the United Kingdom. Crystal Bone, a deep-learning model developed from over 1,000,000 patients in the United States, predicts 1--2 year fracture risk but is not diabetes-specific. Diabetes-specific models are rare: from a cohort of 1,251 patients from Western Australia with type~2 diabetes and 50 hip-fracture cases, a simple five-variable model was developed to predict hip fracture, but it has not been validated.

In contrast to FRAX, our model incorporated a number of risk factors that are specific to diabetes and/or not traditionally considered fracture risk factors. For example, many diabetes-specific codes had high variable importance, including codes associated with diabetes complications and peripheral arterial disease, alongside codes less often considered fracture risk factors such as pneumonia, joint pain, and osteochondritis. Previous attempts to predict fractures with machine learning have likewise surfaced atypical risk factors: a study of over 5,000 men and women from Denmark found that some of the most important model features included dental expenses and the number of TSH measurements.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What the Model Knows vs.\ What the Clinician Knows}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

An important distinction between algorithmic and clinical risk assessment is the nature of the information on which each draws. In clinical practice, the physician's assessment is informed by a targeted history obtained directly from the patient, a physical examination, and the results of tests ordered specifically to characterize the risk of fracture (e.g., DXA, serum calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin~D). This information is current, patient-specific, and actively sought. The clinician can adapt the depth of the investigation in real time, pursue unexpected findings, and integrate information that was never formally recorded.

A machine-learning model trained on administrative claims can only access information that has already been recorded and coded. Through its self-attention mechanism, the transformer encoder can learn associations between co-occurring diagnoses and detect temporal patterns across the visit record; however, it does not interpret these patterns as a clinician would but instead identifies statistical regularities in the coded visit history that are predictive of the outcome.

This asymmetry has an important practical implication. The model is well suited to extracting signal from a large volume of routinely collected coded data. The comparative advantage of the clinician lies in the collection of new, first-hand information that the model cannot access. Combining both sources of information is likely to yield better risk stratification than either alone.

Several variables that a clinician would consider essential are not reliably available in the MarketScan administrative claims used to train BEHRT-LoRA. Race and ethnicity are associated with differences in bone density and fracture incidence, but are poorly captured in commercial claims. BMI is a key input to FRAX and an independent predictor of fracture risk, yet it is infrequently coded in administrative data. The \textit{All of Us} Research Program addresses both of these gaps: as a prospective cohort with demographics and physical measurements reported by participants, it provides race and BMI in addition to the longitudinal diagnosis history that BEHRT-LoRA consumes. Future work incorporating these features may further close the gap between the retrospective signal available to the model and the clinical information that ideally a physician would consult.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Generalization to the All of Us Cohort}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

All three models retained above-chance discrimination on the All of Us cohort, with macro ROC-AUC of 0.758 (BEHRT-LoRA), 0.696 (XGBoost), and 0.680 (NN). The performance ordering observed on MarketScan was preserved on every fracture class, and BEHRT-LoRA held a 0.11--0.14 ROC-AUC advantage over the simpler models on the two intermediate-horizon groups. Discrimination was nonetheless lower than on MarketScan, and AUPRC values for the three fracture classes were small in absolute terms. Several factors plausibly contribute to this attenuation.

First, fracture cases are far rarer in All of Us than in MarketScan: the $<$1-year, 1--5-year, and 5--10-year groups each account for less than 0.4\% of the cohort, compared with 0.7--1.6\% in the MarketScan training data. Per-class AUPRC is bounded above by prevalence, so even a well-discriminating model produces small absolute AUPRC values in this regime.

Second, All of Us enrollment began in 2018. Most participants have shorter post-diabetes follow-up than the MarketScan window allows. Patients who would fracture five to ten years after diabetes onset have largely not been observed long enough, and the late-fracture classes are therefore systematically truncated.

Third, 146 PhecodeX phenotypes observed in All of Us were absent from the MarketScan vocabulary. These tokens were mapped to \texttt{UNK} for BEHRT-LoRA and discarded for XGBoost and the neural network. Any fracture-relevant signal carried by these phenotypes is lost at the input.

Together, these factors point to label and covariate shift rather than a failure of model architecture. They also motivate future work in which the MarketScan-pretrained models are fine-tuned on All of Us sequences with the All of Us vocabulary intact.

To compare BEHRT-LoRA with the current clinical standard, the model was applied to an independent cohort of patients with diabetes at the University of Chicago for whom FRAX hip scores had been clinically assigned along with observed hip fracture outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:uchicago_distribution}). BEHRT-LoRA assigns each patient a probability of belonging to the low-risk class -- that is, the probability of no recorded hip fracture in ten years. The BEHRT risk score was defined as:
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{BEHRT}} = 1 - \hat{P}(\text{class 0}),
\label{eq:behrt-score}
\end{equation}
where class~0 corresponds to the control group (no fracture within ten years). Higher scores indicate a higher predicted fracture risk. The score is continuous and ranges from 0 to 1, serving a role analogous to the FRAX hip percentage.

\paragraph{Risk stratification by decile.}
To assess how well each tool separates patients according to the true risk of fracture, patients were ranked according to their continuous risk score and divided into ten groups of equal size, or deciles. Decile~1 (D1) contains 10\% \ of patients with the lowest scores, and decile~10 (D10) contains 10\% \ of the highest scores. The observed hip fracture rate was then measured within each decile. Figure~\ref{fig:uc_decile} shows these rates for both BEHRT-LoRA and FRAX. Both tools show higher fracture rates in higher deciles: patients ranked in higher deciles by either tool are more likely to fracture than those in lower deciles, confirming that both meaningfully separate patients by true fracture risk.

However, the shape of stratification differed between the two tools. FRAX produced a gradual gradient, with fracture rates steadily increasing from near zero in the lowest deciles to approximately 2.1\% in D10. BEHRT-LoRA concentrated its predictive signal almost entirely within the top decile: fracture rates in D1 through D9 remained near zero, while D10 reached approximately 4.7\%. The vast majority of patients received a low BEHRT score, but those in the top decile carried approximately twice the fracture rate of the highest-risk FRAX group. This distribution suggests that the BEHRT score is strongly skewed toward low values for most patients, with a small proportion receiving markedly elevated scores that correspond to substantially higher observed fracture rates.

\paragraph{Receiver operating characteristic analysis.}
The ROC curves in Figure~\ref{fig:uc_roc} compare discriminative ability in all classification thresholds. BEHRT-LoRA achieved an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.884, compared to 0.765 for the hip of FRAX \%. FRAX is widely considered a moderate discriminator for hip fracture, with AUC values in the literature on diabetes typically reported in the range 0.72--0.78~\citep{Giangregorio2012JBMR,Beaudoin2019OsteoInt}. BEHRT-LoRA achieved this improvement using routinely collected administrative codes alone, without requiring a clinical interview or BMD measurement. This corresponds to an absolute increase of 0.119 in AUROC (0.884 vs.\ 0.765), a relative improvement of approximately 16\%. This gain over FRAX extends the previous literature, suggesting that EHR-derived models can match or exceed FRAX in patients with diabetes.

\paragraph{Precision--recall analysis.}
Because hip fractures are rare (prevalence 0.6\% in this cohort), the ROC curve can be overly optimistic: a model that correctly labels most patients in the majority class will achieve a high AUC even if it performs poorly on cases of greatest clinical importance. The precision--recall (PR) curves in Figure~\ref{fig:uc_pr} provide a more demanding evaluation. A random classifier has an AUPRC equal to the prevalence of the outcome, which corresponds to 0.006 in this cohort. BEHRT-LoRA achieved an AUPRC of 0.125, more than 20 times above this baseline. FRAX achieved an AUPRC of 0.024, four times higher than baseline but more than five times lower than BEHRT-LoRA. At any given level of sensitivity, BEHRT-LoRA raised far fewer false alarms than FRAX, indicating that a clinician acting on BEHRT-LoRA alerts would be correct more often than a clinician acting on FRAX thresholds at the same recall level.

Together, these results demonstrate that BEHRT-LoRA can identify patients with diabetes at elevated risk of hip fracture from the electronic health record alone, without requiring a DXA scan, clinical interview, or active data collection. The model could be continuously applied across the entire population of patients with diabetes within a health system, identifying the small subset of individuals that warrants a formal fracture risk assessment without any additional clinical workload.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limitations}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

Several limitations of the present study warrant recognition. First, training data are administrative claims that record diagnoses for billing purposes rather than clinical completeness. Many variables important for fracture risk assessment are not included, such as body mass index, race and ethnicity, fall history, functional status, visual impairment, and patient-reported symptoms. Second, the BEHRT-LoRA architecture imposes a 512-token limit on patient histories, so for patients with dense clinical activity, the oldest events are discarded. Third, the FRAX comparison was conducted in a single academic center cohort of patients with diabetes at the University of Chicago with a low rate of hip fracture; the generalizability of this comparison to community or rural populations is unknown. Fourth, performance was not assessed in patients who had already been evaluated for osteoporosis. The model may therefore be of greatest utility as a prior-screening step in the broader population of patients with diabetes, before any formal fracture evaluation has been initiated.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Conclusions}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

BEHRT-LoRA, trained in routinely collected administrative claims, identifies patients with diabetes at risk of hip fracture with substantially higher discrimination than gradient-boosted or feedforward neural network architectures. It also outperformed FRAX on both ROC-AUC (0.884 vs.\ 0.765) and AUPRC (0.125 vs.\ 0.024) in an independent external cohort for which clinical FRAX scores were available. The model does not require clinical action beyond what has already been recorded in the electronic health record, making it deployable as a passive, continuous screening tool in a whole population of patients with diabetes. In this workflow, the model identifies the patients that warrant formal evaluation and the clinician determines what to do next. Linking algorithmically derived risk scores to structured clinical follow-up in this manner represents a practical path toward translating these findings into improved fracture prevention.

\section{Methods}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Design and Overview}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims dataset, 2003--2024, to develop and internally validate three machine-learning models for the prediction of hip fracture in patients with diabetes. The prediction task was framed as a four-class classification in which patients were stratified by the time elapsed between the first recorded diagnosis of diabetes and the first recorded hip fracture, with a reference class assigned to patients in whom no diabetes-attributable fracture was observed within ten years.

The three compared models were: (1) BEHRT-LoRA, a BERT-adapted transformer encoder for electronic health records~\citep{Devlin2019BERT,Li2020BEHRT}, trained on the entire MarketScan cohort and fine-tuned for classification using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\citep{Hu2022LoRA}; (2) XGBoost, a gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble~\citep{Chen2016XGBoost}; and (3) a feedforward neural network (\texttt{SparseClassifier}). All three models were then applied, without retraining, to an independent external validation cohort drawn from the \textit{All of Us} Research Program~\citep{AllofUs2019NEJM}, allowing the generalizability assessment to a more demographically diverse population.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data Sources}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims Database}

The Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims dataset contains de-identified administrative health insurance claims from employer-sponsored health plans across the United States. The MarketScan version used in this study covers patients enrolled between January 2003 and January 2024. Data elements included hospital and outpatient diagnosis codes (ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM), continuous enrollment records, and patient demographic information.

\subsubsection{All of Us Research Program}

The \textit{All of Us} Research Program is a prospective longitudinal cohort funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) that has enrolled U.S.\ residents aged 18 years or older from more than 340 recruitment sites throughout the country since May 2018~\citep{AllofUs2019NEJM}. The program explicitly targets historically underrepresented groups in biomedical research, including racial and ethnic minorities and individuals with limited access to health care~\citep{Ramirez2022AllofUs}.

Participant data are organized into a Curated Data Repository (CDR) with three access tiers: Public, Registered, and Controlled. We used the Controlled Tier CDR~v8 (dataset version \texttt{C2024Q3R8}), accessed via the All of Us Researcher Workbench. This release includes data from participants enrolled until October 1, 2023.

\subsubsection{University of Chicago Diabetes Cohort}

Routinely collected electronic health record information (Epic Systems, Verona, WI) from 2011 to 2023 at the University of Chicago was used as an independent cohort for comparison with the FRAX clinical standard. The study was approved by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB).

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Study Cohort and Inclusion Criteria}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

The MarketScan study cohort was constructed by linking three administrative tables and retaining only patients who simultaneously satisfied all eligibility criteria:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Enrollment.} At least one full year of continuous insurance coverage (138{,}879{,}229 eligible enrollment records).
\item \textbf{Demographics.} Age $\geq 20$ years at the start of the first enrollment interval (179{,}415{,}164 eligible demographic records).
\item \textbf{Diagnosis.} At least one claim mapped to a diabetes-specific PhecodeX code~\citep{Shuey2023PhecodeX} (\texttt{EM\_202}, \texttt{EM\_202.1}, or \texttt{EM\_202.2}; all denoting type~2 diabetes mellitus) recorded at age $\geq 20$ years (adult-onset requirement, applied per event to exclude pediatric diagnoses regardless of enrollment age).
\end{itemize}

The inner join of the three tables yielded a final study cohort of $N = 12{,}033{,}245$ patients.

\paragraph{Outcome definition and risk-group assignment.}
For each patient, the diabetes index date $t_{\mathrm{DM}}$ was defined as the date of the first recorded diabetes diagnosis. Hip fracture was identified using the PhecodeX codes \texttt{MS\_745.11} (fracture of the femur) and \texttt{MS\_745.9} (pathological fracture); the outcome index date $t_{\mathrm{HF}}$ was defined as the date of the first such code. We defined $\Delta t = t_{\mathrm{HF}} - t_{\mathrm{DM}}$ (in days) when a fracture occurred after the diabetes index, and $\Delta t = \infty$ otherwise. The patients were assigned to one of the four mutually exclusive risk groups:
\begin{equation}
y =
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{No-fracture control,} \\[2pt]
1, & 366 \leq \Delta t \leq 1{,}825 \quad (1\text{--}5\text{ years}), \\[2pt]
2, & 1 \leq \Delta t \leq 365 \quad (<\!1\text{ year}), \\[2pt]
3, & 1{,}826 \leq \Delta t \leq 3{,}650 \quad (5\text{--}10\text{ years}). \\[2pt]
\end{cases}
\label{eq:risk-groups}
\end{equation}
The control class therefore aggregates three subgroups: patients without recorded fractures, patients with fractures occurring on or before $t_{\mathrm{DM}}$, and patients with fractures occurring more than ten years after $t_{\mathrm{DM}}$. These subgroups were collapsed into a single reference class because hip fracture is a rare outcome and because an interval of more than ten years is rare. The cohort counts for each group are presented in Table~\ref{tab:groups}.

To qualify for the study, patients with diabetes had to be seen at the University of Chicago medical center at least once per year, on average, for diabetes (ICD-9: 249--250; ICD-10: E08--E11). \subsection{Feature Representation}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA: EHR Token Sequences}

\paragraph{Code mapping.}
The raw diagnosis codes ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM were mapped to PhecodeX phenotypes~\citep{Shuey2023PhecodeX}, which group semantically related ICD codes into single clinical concepts. This mapping reduces vocabulary size and improves portability between ICD versions and between data sources.

\paragraph{Sequence construction.}
For each patient, all diagnosis events were sorted chronologically and restricted to those that occurred strictly before $t_{\mathrm{HF}}$. The two phenotypes of hip-fracture (\texttt{MS\_745.11} and \texttt{MS\_745.9}) were excluded from the input sequence in both case and control patients to prevent leakage of the results. Events occurring on the same calendar day were grouped into a single visit; a separator token (\texttt{SEP}) was inserted between consecutive visits and appended at the end of each record. The patient's age at each event was computed as the difference between the event calendar year and the patient's birth year. Sex was treated as a static patient-level attribute and was assigned uniformly across all positions in the sequence.

\paragraph{Token vocabularies and embeddings.}
Each input element in a patient's history---a diagnosis, an age, or a sex label---is referred to as a \textit{token}. Unlike a real text corpus, an EHR sequence contains several heterogeneous token types, each drawn from its own vocabulary (Table~\ref{tab:vocabularies}).

A transformer cannot operate on tokens directly; each token must be represented as a fixed-length real-valued vector, termed an \textit{embedding}. For every entry in each vocabulary, the model maintains one such vector. These vectors are not specified a priori; they are initialized randomly and updated during training such that tokens with similar clinical or contextual meaning come to occupy nearby regions of the embedding space.

Two embeddings encode structural rather than clinical information. The \textit{segment embedding} indicates which visit a diagnosis belongs to: it alternates between two values at visit boundaries, so that all diagnoses within the same visit share the same segment value. The \textit{position embedding} indicates the order of visits: it increments once per visit rather than once per diagnosis, so that the model attends to the sequence of visits rather than to the sequence of individual codes.

At each position $i$ in the sequence, the five embedding vectors are summed to form a single combined input vector:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_i \;=\; \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{phen}}(p_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{age}}(a_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{sex}}(s_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{seg}}(g_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{pos}}(v_i),
\label{eq:embedding-sum}
\end{equation}
where $p_i, a_i, s_i, g_i, v_i$ denote the phenotype, age, sex, segment, and position tokens at position $i$, and each $\mathbf{E}_{(\cdot)}$ is a learnable lookup table mapping a vocabulary index to a $d$-dimensional vector. The transformer encoder consumes the full patient history as the ordered sequence $(\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_L)$.

\paragraph{Sequence length and aggregation.}
The encoder processes a maximum of 512 positions per patient, a fixed architectural limit~\citep{Devlin2019BERT}. The first position is reserved for a special \texttt{CLS} token prepended to every sequence. For patients with longer histories, the oldest events were truncated and only the 511 most recent events were retained, assuming that recent clinical history is more predictive of near-term outcomes. The \texttt{CLS} token carries no clinical meaning; it serves as an aggregation point. By the final layer of the transformer, the vector in the \texttt{CLS} position has attended to every other position in the sequence and constitutes a fixed-length summary of the entire patient history~\citep{Devlin2019BERT}. This vector is the input to the classification head.

\subsubsection{XGBoost and Feedforward Neural Network: One-Hot Feature Vectors}

For the gradient-boosted ensemble (XGBoost) and the feedforward neural network, each patient's clinical history was represented as a sparse binary feature vector; chronological information was not preserved.

The feature space consisted of $K = 3{,}304$ unique PhecodeX phenotypes observed in the MarketScan training cohort. For each patient, element $k$ of the vector was set to 1 if phenotype $k$ appeared in the patient's record, and 0 otherwise. To prevent outcome leakage, only phenotype codes recorded strictly before $t_{\mathrm{HF}}$ were included for case patients (groups~1--3); for control patients, no temporal restriction was applied. The two hip-fracture phenotypes (\texttt{MS\_745.11} and \texttt{MS\_745.9}) were removed from the feature vocabulary for all patients. The resulting design matrix contained 12{,}033{,}245 rows and 3{,}304 columns, with a density of approximately 1.22\%, and was stored in Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) format for memory efficiency.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model Architectures and Training}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA}

BEHRT (BERT for Electronic Health Records)~\citep{Li2020BEHRT} is a transformer encoder adapted from the original BERT architecture~\citep{Devlin2019BERT,Vaswani2017Attention} for EHR data. Our implementation was first pre-trained on the full MarketScan corpus using a masked language-modeling (MLM) objective and subsequently fine-tuned for four-class hip-fracture risk prediction.

\paragraph{Encoder architecture.}
The encoder follows the standard BERT design: a stack of transformer blocks, each comprising multi-head self-attention followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, with residual connections and layer normalization at each sub-layer~\citep{Vaswani2017Attention}. After the final transformer layer, the vector at the \texttt{CLS} position is passed to a linear classification head that produces logits over the four risk classes.

\paragraph{Architecture hyperparameter optimization.}
Architecture hyperparameters were selected by Bayesian optimization (Optuna~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna}, Tree-structured Parzen Estimator sampler); the full search space and selected configuration are reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp:hyperparams}.

\paragraph{Pre-training.}
Pre-training used the MLM objective~\citep{Devlin2019BERT}: at each step, 15\% of the tokens in each sequence were randomly selected; of these, 80\% were replaced with \texttt{[MASK]}, 10\% were replaced with a random token drawn from the vocabulary, and 10\% were left unchanged. In all three cases, the model was trained to predict the original token. This mixed corruption scheme prevents the model from learning representations that depend on the presence of the \texttt{[MASK]} symbol, which would otherwise degrade fine-tuning performance, where no masking is applied. Formally, given a sequence $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_L)$, a corrupted sequence $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$, and the set of masked positions $\mathcal{M}$, the pre-training loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MLM}} \;=\; -\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log P_\theta\!\left(x_i \,\big|\, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}\right),
\label{eq:mlm}
\end{equation}
where $P_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ denotes the model's predicted distribution over the phenotype vocabulary at position $i$. Pre-training used a 90/10 train/validation split, up to 100 epochs, and early stopping with patience of 10 evaluations (4 evaluations per epoch). Training was distributed across four GPUs with an effective batch size of 1{,}024.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning with LoRA.}
After pre-training, the MLM prediction head was discarded and the BERT backbone was frozen. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\citep{Hu2022LoRA} was injected into the query and value projection matrices of every self-attention layer. LoRA replaces a frozen weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ with the parameterization
\begin{equation}
W \;=\; W_0 + \Delta W \;=\; W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,B A,
\qquad
A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d},\ \ B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\ \ r \ll d,
\label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is initialized from a Gaussian and $B$ is initialized to zero, ensuring that $\Delta W = 0$ at the start of fine-tuning and that the model output is initially unchanged from the pre-trained baseline. Only $A$ and $B$ are updated during fine-tuning; $W_0$ remains frozen. Because $r \ll d$, the number of trainable parameters per adapted layer is reduced from $d^2$ to $2rd$. We used $r = 16$, $\alpha = 32$ (effective scale $\alpha/r = 2$), and a LoRA dropout of 0.1, leaving approximately 1--2\% of the total model parameters trainable. A linear classification head was appended to the frozen backbone and trained jointly with the LoRA adapters.

Fine-tuning used AdamW~\citep{Loshchilov2019AdamW} with learning rate $2\times 10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01, linear warmup over the first 10\% of training steps, and gradient accumulation over 4 steps. Class imbalance was addressed by a class-weighted cross-entropy loss, with weights inversely proportional to class frequency:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} \;=\; -\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} w_{y_n}\, \log \hat{p}_{n,y_n},
\qquad
w_c \;\propto\; \frac{1}{N_c},\quad \sum_{c=0}^{C-1} w_c = C,
\label{eq:weighted-ce}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{p}_{n,c}$ is the predicted probability that patient $n$ belongs to class $c$, $y_n \in \{0,\dots,C{-}1\}$ is the true label, $N_c$ is the number of training patients in class $c$, and $C = 4$. Training was conducted for up to 50 epochs, with early stopping (patience 10 epochs) based on validation loss.

\subsubsection{XGBoost}

A gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble was trained using XGBoost~\citep{Chen2016XGBoost}. XGBoost constructs trees sequentially, with each new tree fitted to the gradient (and Hessian) of the loss with respect to the current ensemble's predictions; the final prediction is the sum of all tree outputs passed through a softmax function for multi-class classification.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
The training cohort comprised approximately 99\% controls and $<\!1\%$ of patients in the three fracture classes combined. To prevent the model from collapsing to the majority class, we (i)~down-sampled the control class to 600{,}000 patients (from over 10 million) and (ii)~applied the class-weighting scheme of Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-ce} during training. Validation and test sets retained their natural class distributions to preserve unbiased estimates of generalization performance.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter optimization.}
Hyperparameters were tuned with Optuna~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna} using 5-fold stratified cross-validation; the search procedure and selected configuration are reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp:hyperparams}.

\subsubsection{Feedforward Neural Network}

A feedforward neural network (\texttt{SparseClassifier}) was trained on the same sparse one-hot feature matrix used for XGBoost. The architecture accepts a 3{,}304-dimensional input and passes it through three fully connected hidden layers of dimensions 512, 256, and 128, each followed by batch normalization, a ReLU activation, and dropout. A final linear layer maps the 128-dimensional hidden representation to four class logits, from which class probabilities are obtained via the softmax function.

\paragraph{Class imbalance.}
We applied the same strategy as for XGBoost: the control class was down-sampled to 600{,}000 patients, and a class-weighted cross-entropy loss (Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-ce}) was applied during training.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter optimization.}
Hyperparameters were selected with Optuna~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna}; the search procedure and selected configuration are reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp:hyperparams}.

%% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{External Validation in All of Us}
%% ----------------------------------------------------------

All three models trained on MarketScan were applied without modification to the All of Us validation cohort. Model weights were not updated during validation. Cohort and feature construction followed the same pipelines used for MarketScan, with the adaptations described below.

\subsubsection{BEHRT-LoRA}

Diagnosis sequences were constructed using the same procedure as for MarketScan. ICD-to-PhecodeX mapping was executed in BigQuery on approximately 123~million condition records. To ensure that phenotype tokens mapped to the same embedding vectors as during pre-training, all All of Us sequences were tokenized using the MarketScan-derived vocabulary; phenotypes present in All of Us but absent from the MarketScan vocabulary were mapped to the \texttt{UNK} token.

All three models were evaluated on the same set of All of Us participants with diabetes, so that their performance metrics are directly comparable.

\subsubsection{XGBoost and Feedforward Neural Network}

A sparse binary feature matrix was constructed using the same procedure as for MarketScan, restricted to the same 3{,}304 PhecodeX columns used at training. The 146 PhecodeX phenotypes present in All of Us but absent from the MarketScan feature set were discarded; columns observed in MarketScan but absent from All of Us were retained as all-zero vectors to preserve input dimensionality.

\section{Hyperparameter optimization}
\label{supp:hyperparams}

\paragraph{BEHRT-LoRA.}
Architecture hyperparameters were selected by Bayesian optimization (Optuna~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna}, Tree-structured Parzen Estimator sampler, 30 trials), trained on a subset of 100{,}000 patients for five epochs per trial. The search space comprised: hidden dimension $d \in \{128, 192, 256, 288, 384\}$; number of layers $\in \{2,\dots,8\}$; attention heads $\in \{4, 6, 8, 12\}$; feed-forward intermediate dimension $\in \{256, 512, 768, 1{,}024\}$; dropout $\in [0.05, 0.30]$; and learning rate $\in [10^{-5}, 5\times 10^{-4}]$.

\paragraph{XGBoost.}
Hyperparameters were tuned with Optuna~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna} (TPE sampler, 60 trials, 4 trials in parallel across 4 GPUs) using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the down-sampled training set, with mean cross-entropy loss across folds as the objective. The selected configuration was: maximum tree depth, 8; learning rate, 0.282; row subsampling ratio, 0.924; column subsampling ratio, 0.666; minimum child weight, 11; $\gamma = 1.138$; L1 regularization $\alpha = 2 \times 10^{-6}$; L2 regularization $\lambda = 2.256$; and 64 histogram bins. The final model was trained on the full down-sampled training set with these settings for up to 500 boosting rounds, with early stopping (patience 50 rounds) on the held-out validation set.

\paragraph{Feedforward neural network.}
Hyperparameters were selected with Optuna (TPE sampler, 30 trials)~\citep{Akiba2019Optuna} on a stratified subset of 150{,}000 training patients, using an 80/20 train/validation split per trial and early stopping after four epochs without improvement. The tuned parameters were learning rate, weight decay, dropout rate, and batch size. The selected configuration was: learning rate, $2.98 \times 10^{-4}$; weight decay, $9.59 \times 10^{-4}$; dropout, 0.306; batch size, 1{,}024. The final model was trained on the complete down-sampled training set with these settings for up to 60 epochs, using AdamW~\citep{Loshchilov2019AdamW} with a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler (factor 0.5, patience 3 epochs) and early stopping on cross-entropy validation loss (patience 10 epochs).
